# The Trees Nobody Planted (Niger, history channel)

The farmer-managed natural regeneration story: Tony Rinaudo and Niger's ~200 million trees.

## Facts (checked Sept 2026)
- Drought in the Sahel from the late 1960s, peaking in the early 1970s; famine again in the 1980s.
- Tony Rinaudo, Australian agronomist, in Niger from the early 1980s. Most seedlings he planted died. Around 1983 he realized the "bushes" in fields were living tree stumps.
- Farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR): keep the strongest shoots on each stump, prune the rest.
- Result: about 200 million trees across about 5 million hectares; roughly 500,000 extra tonnes of grain a year, enough for about 2.5 million people.
- Don't claim the Great Green Wall is longer than the Great Wall of China (it isn't: ~8,000 km vs ~8,850 km).

## Approved audio version (Liam, ~60 s)
This is what `studio/niger/mix.py` records, with SFX cues. Gemini rated it 9/10 catchiness,
9/10 hook, 10/10 clarity. Liam was chosen over Puck.

> Two hundred million trees. In one of the driest places on Earth. And not a single one was planted.
> Fifty years ago, this land was dying.
> South of the Sahara lies the Sahel. For centuries, farmers grew their crops right next to the trees. Until they made one small decision.
> They cut them down. For farmland. For firewood. Nobody thought it mattered.
> Then, in the early nineteen seventies, the rain stopped.
> And suddenly, everyone found out what those trees had been doing all along.
> The wind ripped the soil right off the fields. Crops died. Cattle died. Millions went hungry. And the desert kept coming.
> Then an Australian named Tony Rinaudo showed up with a plan. Plant new trees. He planted thousands.
> Almost all of them died.
> He was about to give up. Until one day, he looked down at a scruffy little bush, and realized it wasn't a bush.
> It was a tree. Cut down years ago. With its roots still alive underground.
> The forest had never left. It was hiding under the dirt, waiting.
> So farmers stopped planting, and started protecting. And twenty years later, two hundred million trees were back.
> Enough to feed two and a half million more people.

## GeoGlobeTales-style rewrite (approved, not yet recorded)

> Niger. Right on the edge of the Sahara, one of the driest countries on Earth. And yet today, it has around 200 million more trees than it did in the 1980s. Funny thing is, almost nobody planted them.
>
> 1970s. South of the Sahara, in a region called the Sahel, farmers grow millet between trees that have stood there for centuries. But the population is growing fast, so people need more fields and more firewood. And those trees are just in the way. So they cut them down. Problem solved.
>
> Well, not exactly. Because it turns out those trees were holding the soil together and shading the crops. So when a huge drought hits, the wind simply blows the fields away. Crops fail, cattle die, and millions of people go hungry.
>
> So the world does what it always does. Let's plant trees! Millions of seedlings, a lot of money… and almost all of them die.
>
> 1983. An Australian agronomist named Tony Rinaudo is driving through Niger, ready to give up and go home. He stops, looks at a scruffy little bush in a field, and realizes it's not a bush. It's a tree. Cut down years ago, but its roots are still alive, and it keeps trying to grow back. And every year, farmers chop it down again, thinking it's a weed.
>
> So the solution isn't planting new trees. It's just… to stop killing the old ones. Farmers keep the strongest shoot on each stump, and let it grow.
>
> Over the next 20 years, they bring back 200 million trees. One of the biggest environmental comebacks in Africa, and it all started with a guy looking at a bush.

Techniques used: fact-plus-problem opening, a year starting each scene, present tense, sarcasm
("Problem solved. Well, not exactly."), people's inner thoughts ("Let's plant trees!"),
understatement, punchline ending.

## How the script evolved (and why)
1. **Great Green Wall draft** with countryball jokes: rejected, it didn't match the reference format (one narrator, no dialogue, one straight line to "it worked").
2. **Same format as the reference**: too close to its exact phrases ("Look,", "literally", "a disaster caused by humans themselves").
3. **Own wording**: too many "writer" phrases ("quietly did a huge job", "stunning").
4. **Plain version**: too flat.
5. **Tension version**: better, but gave answers straight away and asked too many questions.
6. **Open-loop version**: hook with a mystery, reveal held to the end. Became the audio above after tightening the hook.
7. **GeoGlobeTales version**: after studying 5 of his scripts.

Visual ideas: stickman farmers chopping trees, trees vanishing one by one, cracked earth, a goat
eating a seedling, an x-ray view of big roots under a tiny bush, the map slowly turning green.
